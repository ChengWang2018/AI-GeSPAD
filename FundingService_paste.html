Quantum technologies such as the quantum internet and photonic quantum computers carry information on single particles of light (photons), usually at the 1550 nanometre wavelength used by today's fibre-optic networks. Detecting these single photons reliably is essential, but the best current detectors either need cooling to a few degrees above absolute zero or are expensive and cannot be built with standard silicon chip technology. This limits how far, how cheaply and how sustainably quantum networks can scale.

A promising alternative is a germanium-on-silicon single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD), in which germanium absorbs the infrared photon and silicon amplifies the tiny electrical signal into a measurable pulse. Such detectors can be made in silicon foundries and fed by an on-chip waveguide, but no one has yet shown one that is both efficient at 1550 nanometres and quiet enough (few false "dark" counts) without cryogenics. Design is hard for three reasons:

(1) efficiency and noise depend on more than ten interacting choices of layer shape, doping and electric field;

(2) each device simulation takes minutes to hours, so conventional methods explore only a small part of the possibilities;

(3) the physics models and the fabrication process are uncertain, so a design that looks best on paper may fail once made.

The central question is whether artificial intelligence (AI) can find detector designs whose good performance survives these uncertainties, with far fewer simulations than conventional design.

AI-GeSPAD pairs Dr. Xin Yi, a detector physicist who made the first compound-semiconductor avalanche photodiode of the type used in telecoms with lower noise than silicon, and leads an industry partnership on 1550 nanometre detectors, with Dr. Cheng Wang, whose uncertainty-aware safety-testing methods for mobility autonomy have been adopted by industry. Its three objectives answer these difficulties:

(1) build an automated, multi-level simulation pipeline calibrated against real detectors measured at Heriot-Watt, which also quantifies the physics uncertainty;

(2) use uncertainty-aware AI that learns directly from the simulated device structure (a graph neural network) to search the design space efficiently and map the best trade-offs between efficiency and noise at 230 K, reachable without cryogenics; each design is then verified by an AI search for the uncertain conditions under which it would fail, so that we keep only designs whose performance survives what we do not know, and the best are checked from 250 K up to room temperature;

(3) turn what the AI has learned into physically meaningful design rules, and check the best designs independently with the most accurate simulations and simple test devices.

The project will deliver a shortlist of robust detector designs ready for fabrication, new design rules, an open-source AI-assisted device-design toolkit and benchmark dataset, and a roadmap towards building and testing the detectors with the UK's Integrated Quantum Networks Hub. It aims to reach the performance of reference designs with at least five times fewer accurate simulations than conventional sampling.

The toolkit gives UK researchers a reusable, trustworthy way to apply AI to semiconductor device design, including other infrared detectors, lasers and modulators. Telecom-wavelength detectors that are cheaper, chip-integrated and need no cryogenics would lower the cost and energy use of quantum networks and support the UK photonics and quantum supply chain, in line with the UK's National Quantum Strategy, AI Opportunities Action Plan and AI for Science ambitions.

AI-GeSPAD pairs a domain expert, Dr. Xin Yi (Assistant Professor, EPSRC Quantum Technology Fellow, Institute of Photonics and Quantum Sciences, IPaQS), with an AI expert, Dr. Cheng Wang (Assistant Professor, MSCA Fellow, RoboSafe lab), both at Heriot-Watt University (HWU). Contributions to the generation of new ideas, tools, methodologies or knowledge

Dr. Yi has worked on avalanche-device physics since 2015 and has led an EPSRC Fellowship (2022), an EPSRC New Investigator Award (2024) and an Innovate UK Knowledge Transfer Partnership (2026) as Principal Investigator, publishing 19 journal and 4 conference papers on avalanche detectors, mostly as lead or corresponding author. In 2019 he demonstrated the first III–V avalanche photodiode (APD) lattice-matched to InP with lower excess noise than silicon at room temperature, resolving a four-decade challenge and catalysing international work on low-noise APDs [1], followed by valence-band-engineered APDs [2]. Most relevant here, he established temperature-dependent impact-ionisation models [3], breakdown-probability simulations for InP and Ge-on-Si SPADs [4], multiplication models for non-uniform electric fields and field analysis of Ge-on-Si SPADs [5], and reduced afterpulsing in Ge-on-Si SPADs [6]. These models and his measured devices form the physics core of WP1, and his characterisation expertise underpins validation in WP3.

Dr. Wang has worked on the safety assessment of AI-driven systems since 2017. At TU Darmstadt he developed statistical methods that quantify and reduce uncertainty in simulation-based assessment [7] and the Silent Testing method [8], adopted by Continental, as a junior leader in the German VVM project (€47m) with BMW and Bosch. At DJI he led the open AD4CHE trajectory dataset [9], now used by more than 400 organisations, and at the University of Edinburgh he worked on explainable AI, releasing the first open dataset of human explanations of driving decisions. His Marie Skłodowska-Curie Fellowship (2023, top 12% in engineering) produced open generative methods for rare scenarios [10], and he leads the safety task of the European Horizon Europe AEROSUB project (€12m), which he created by transferring his methods to underwater robots. AI-GeSPAD transfers these tools to device design: uncertainty-aware surrogates and calibration (WP1, WP2), rare-failure search for the challenger (WP2), explainability (WP3) and open data release, and AEROSUB shows he can carry them into a new domain.

The project is mainly computational, with in-project laboratory measurements and simple test-device fabrication at Heriot-Watt, and involves no human participants, personal data, animals or biological materials. Its main ethical and responsible-innovation considerations are dual use, the trustworthiness of AI-generated scientific claims, research integrity, laboratory safety and environmental cost. We will address these using the AREA framework (anticipate, reflect, engage, act) throughout the project.

Dual use and security. Infrared single-photon detectors are used in quantum communications and also in LIDAR and imaging, including defence applications. We will work with Heriot-Watt's Trusted Research and export-control advisers before releasing designs or code. We will review the release of detailed device designs against UK strategic export-control lists, and share general methods and tools openly while keeping release-sensitive design specifics under controlled access if advised.

Trustworthy AI for science. A key risk of AI in science is confident but wrong results. Our design embeds safeguards drawn from safety-critical AI: calibrated uncertainty on every prediction, adversarial falsification of candidate designs, independent blind validation with high-fidelity physics simulation, and explainable design rules checked by a domain expert. We will report negative results and the limits of the surrogate models transparently, and will not claim device performance that has not been validated by high-fidelity simulation and, where possible, by our own measurements of existing devices, test structures and test diodes. Measured results will be reported alongside the predictions, including any disagreement.

Laboratory work. Temperature-dependent measurements in Dr. Yi's laboratory and fabrication in the EPS cleanroom follow Heriot-Watt's risk assessments, cleanroom training and chemical-safety procedures, and all measured data are recorded with full metadata.

Research integrity and openness. All code, simulation inputs and outputs, and model versions will be recorded so that every result can be reproduced. Generative AI tools will not be used to produce scientific results, and any use in writing will follow UKRI policy and be acknowledged.

Environmental sustainability. Large simulation and training campaigns use energy. Multi-fidelity active learning is chosen partly to minimise the number of expensive simulations. We will log compute hours and estimated energy use and report them in publications.

Equality, diversity and inclusion, and people. The PDRA post will be advertised openly with flexible-working options, and the PDRA will receive structured career development and credit (for example lead authorship on the D2 journal paper).

Data. No personal or confidential data are involved.

A high-risk, untested idea. AI-GeSPAD tests an unproven idea: that AI, coupled with physics simulation, can find semiconductor device designs that experienced device physicists have not found, in a space where success depends on rare, poorly understood effects. Several things could fail. Surrogate models may struggle with sharp transitions such as avalanche breakdown and tunnelling onset. Calibrating simulations to the few existing devices may leave large uncertainty. The target itself (efficient, low-noise 1550 nm single-photon detection in a waveguide-coupled Ge-on-Si device near room temperature) may be beyond what the physics allows. Transferring falsification methods to semiconductor devices has, as far as we know, not been attempted before.

A transformative reward. If it succeeds, the project would provide the first credible, robustness-verified design route to CMOS-compatible, chip-integrated telecom single-photon detectors that work without cryogenics. That would be a step change for scalable quantum networks and photonic quantum computing, where detector cost, cooling and integration are key bottlenecks. The method is equally important: a trustworthy design loop, in which every AI-proposed design is verified against the conditions that could make it fail, could become a standard tool for semiconductor device research. It could shorten design cycles and reduce costly fabrication runs for detectors, lasers and modulators across the UK semiconductor and photonics sector.

Managing the risk. Every stage produces useful outputs even if the headline target is not met. The calibrated simulation pipeline and dataset (month 4), the robust Pareto map of achievable trade-offs (month 9), and the failure-mechanism catalogue from the challenger (month 10) each advance understanding. The challenger reuses the trained surrogate, so if earlier work slips it can shrink to a worst-case check of the final designs without losing the robustness evidence. A clear demonstration that the target is physically infeasible would itself redirect the field's effort.

Alignment with national strategy. The project directly implements the AI Opportunities Action Plan's ambition to accelerate scientific discovery with AI (DSIT, January 2025), and the AI for Science Strategy's focus on AI-enabled scientific workflows, data and skills, in which quantum technologies are a named priority (DSIT, November 2025). It supports the Modern Industrial Strategy, which prioritises frontier technologies including AI, quantum and semiconductors (HM Government, June 2025), and the National Quantum Strategy and Quantum Missions on networks and computing (DSIT, 2023).

The right scheme for this work. This call's explicit pairing of AI and domain experts, its 12-month exploratory format and its emphasis on feasibility evidence and roadmaps fit the project exactly. Other routes suit later stages: standard EPSRC responsive-mode panels review within one discipline, where a quantum-device panel would see the AI as unproven and an AI panel would see the device domain as outside its remit; Innovate UK funds commercialisation of validated designs, which this project will create; and the IQN Hub's committed work programme focuses on established detector technologies. Positive results will then support a larger follow-on proposal to the next UKRI Quantum Missions call.
